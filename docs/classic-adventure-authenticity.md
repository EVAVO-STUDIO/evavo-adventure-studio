# Authentic classic-adventure production principles

## Study production grammar, not protected content

Adventure Studio can learn from the production discipline of early-1990s narrative adventures without copying a commercial game's characters, story, scene layouts, dialogue, maps, logos, interface artwork or puzzle solutions.

The reusable lessons are structural:

- compose at final native resolution;
- establish a controlled palette and value hierarchy;
- preserve readable silhouettes and interaction lanes;
- build interfaces around the fiction and interaction model;
- make rooms change with time, story state and consequence;
- connect clues, puzzles, chapters and travel into one causality graph;
- storyboard cinematics as authored shots with deterministic outcomes;
- reward observation and experimentation with specific feedback.

## Native-resolution composition

A convincing 320 × 200 or 640 × 480 scene is designed for that canvas from the first thumbnail. It is not a detailed modern painting reduced at export.

A production-ready scene should establish:

1. the dominant value mass;
2. the walkable stage or cinematic eye path;
3. the player-character silhouette;
4. exits and major threats;
5. consequential props;
6. decorative story detail.

The visual hierarchy must still work when the image is viewed at native size with no hotspot overlay.

## Palette architecture

A palette is a dramatic system, not only a technical colour limit. Each project should define:

- anchor shadows;
- material families;
- skin and costume separation;
- environmental light colours;
- interaction accents;
- interface accents;
- restricted highlights.

Dithering should describe material, atmosphere or a deliberate transition. Applying one noise pattern over the whole image destroys material distinction and makes the result look processed rather than painted.

## Character readability and animation

Classic-scale actors need strong silhouettes, consistent foot placement and readable held objects. Costume shape, posture and motion rhythm should identify a character before portraits or text labels do.

Animation should prioritise:

- anticipation;
- contact;
- reaction;
- recovery;
- held key poses;
- loops that begin and end cleanly.

More frames do not automatically create better animation. A small number of intentional poses usually reads more clearly at native resolution than interpolated motion with weak silhouettes.

## Interface identity

Parser, verb list, icon bar, two-button, verb coin and context interaction are different design grammars. The editor should let each project own its interface layout, cursor language, bitmap typography, inventory treatment, score policy and dialogue presentation.

The interface should not solve observation by permanently highlighting every hotspot. Assistance can exist, but it must remain an authored accessibility policy rather than the default visual language.

## Puzzle fairness and dramatic purpose

A puzzle should advance at least one of:

- story;
- access;
- character understanding;
- relationship;
- world knowledge;
- mastery of a game rule.

The player should understand the current problem before encountering its solution casually. Required puzzles need guaranteed clue delivery, specific failure feedback and a recovery path. Dangerous actions need intelligible warning before irreversible consequence.

Multiple solutions are valuable when they express skills, relationships or role-playing identity. They should not be cosmetic aliases that produce identical feedback without recognising the player's approach.

## Geography, time and scene state

A map is useful when it explains the fiction's geography and progression. It should show meaningful routes, changing access and travel consequence rather than behaving like a modern navigation application.

Locations should evolve through:

- chapter or day changes;
- weather and lighting;
- character presence;
- object state;
- new dialogue topics;
- visible consequences of solved puzzles;
- alternate routes or hazards.

Revisiting a room should reveal state, not merely repeat the same static composition.

## Cinematic direction

Cutscenes should be planned as a sequence of shots, not a block of dialogue attached to a camera pan. Each shot needs a purpose:

- establish geography;
- reveal an object;
- define an eye line;
- change power between characters;
- deliver a reaction;
- transition back to play.

Skippable cinematics must apply the same canonical completion actions as watched cinematics. Presentation can differ; game state cannot.

## Editor implications

A complete authoring environment therefore needs connected surfaces for:

- creative direction and authenticity rules;
- world map and chapter progression;
- scene composition, walkable geometry, depth and occlusion;
- object states and interactions;
- actor animation and portraits;
- bitmap fonts and interface skins;
- dialogue topics and branching;
- puzzle and clue causality;
- shot-based cutscene storyboards and executable sequences;
- deterministic playtest inspection and validation.

Adventure Design Director provides the connective production layer. Existing Studio workspaces remain the canonical editors for their specialised runtime documents.

## Primary-source research basis

The production rules above are derived from reusable lessons documented by original creators and rights holders, not from copying protected game content.

- Ron Gilbert's 1989 design article, [Why Adventure Games Suck](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck/), argues for clear objectives and sub-goals, survivable danger, presenting the problem before its solution, story-advancing puzzles, incremental reward, logical causality and rewarding player intent. Adventure Design Director turns those principles into puzzle, clue, recovery and feedback checks.
- Gilbert's [Monkey Island design notebook notes](https://grumpygamer.com/monkey_island_design_scribblings/) show that main flow and individual ideas were iterated rather than arriving fully formed. The editor therefore keeps puzzle order, map progression and chapter intent editable and undoable instead of locking them into a one-pass template.
- Double Fine's official [Day of the Tentacle Remastered](https://www.doublefine.com/games/day-of-the-tentacle-remastered) material preserves classic graphics, audio and interface as independently selectable systems and includes concept art and original-creator commentary. Adventure Studio likewise treats art direction, interface, audio and runtime behaviour as connected but separately authored disciplines.
- The official [Day of the Tentacle credits](https://www.doublefine.com/games/day-of-the-tentacle-remastered/credits) distinguish lead art, background styling, character design, animation, music and interactive-music responsibilities. The production brief therefore avoids collapsing every visual and audio decision into one generic style field.
- Double Fine's [Making of Grim Fandango Remastered](https://www.doublefine.com/games/grim-fandango-remastered/making) documents recovery of archived material and involvement of original creators. That supports keeping production intent, concept decisions and canonical game state reviewable alongside final assets.

These references justify the architecture and review criteria. They do not license reproducing commercial scenes, characters, dialogue, maps, interfaces, music, art or puzzle solutions.
